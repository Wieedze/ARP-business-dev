# Liveness feeds sell to gatekeepers, not readers

The hole is real but narrow, and nobody has yet shown it can be sold. Nobody publishes a neutral, reproducible, longitudinal record of whether agent tools respond and whether they changed behind a stable name, in a form that gatekeepers (enterprise registries, payment rails, security proxies) could consume. The evidence that this is needed is strong: about half of remote MCP servers fail an unauthenticated handshake, 40.6% of multi-version registry servers made silent semantic changes, and every independent ERC-8004 liveness study finds callability between single digits and at most 15%. The evidence that anyone pays for it is absent. In every adjacent market, readers never pay for ratings. Money comes from enterprises buying enforcement (JFrog, Chainguard, Socket), from rated parties buying visibility (Trustpilot, G2), or from scores that gate a financial decision (Dun & Bradstreet). Your demand hypothesis survives: I found no measured evidence of agents querying registries programmatically at scale, but that means not found, not proven absent, because no registry publishes query volumes. Your product, as it sits in the repo today, does not yet occupy the hole. The liveness gate is not wired into any listing, the 53 catalogued tools are GitHub URLs, so the on-chain signal is "maintained" rather than "reachable", and on mainnet 0 of 186 `uses` edges come from an identified agent. Two of your beliefs need correcting. Kleros Curate is a token-curated registry (TCR) that has sustained, with subsidy. Stake can stay informative when an external, checkable outcome settles it.

**Conventions.** Every source below was read on 2026-10-29. Where the source's own data date differs, it is given in the text. **(M)** marks a figure measured by a third party with a stated method. **(SR)** marks a self-reported figure from the vendor or someone it paid. **(Repo)** marks a figure from your own repository, which I did not re-run. Repo figures come from a due diligence of `github.com/Wieedze/ARP` at branch `feat/agent-panel-tabs`, commit `b5682d9` (2026-10-29). That review could not reach the Intuition indexers, the GitHub API or npm stats, so on-chain figures there are the repo's claims.

Three rules fall out. First, **the reader of a public rating never pays**. Second, money arrives through one of three doors: an enterprise enforcing a policy at install or call time, a rated party buying visibility (with BBB showing the credibility cost of that door, [CNNMoney](https://money.cnn.com/2015/09/30/news/better-business-bureau-millions/)), or a score gating a financial decision. Third, **a verification that can be automated and offered free by a credible neutral party drifts to a price of zero**, which is exactly what your "anyone can re-verify liveness" design invites. A liveness probe is the Let's Encrypt DV certificate of agent trust: valuable, necessary, and very hard to charge for on its own.

The evidence against selling the score is direct. Stacklok, a funded team, built a free package trust score and moved on to MCP runtime tooling. Phylum's scoring ended as assets inside a larger vendor. Rated Labs ("reputation for machines") still operates with paid APIs but shows no pricing, customers or revenue ([rated.network](https://www.rated.network)). The open MCP directories disclose no revenue, monetize through hosting (Glama, Smithery) or sponsored slots (mcpservers.org shows sponsor-labeled entries, [mcpservers.org](https://mcpservers.org/)), and the largest consolidation event was a runtime company (Arcade) buying a directory.

### Demand: your hypothesis holds, with two caveats

Developer demand is large and measured: `@modelcontextprotocol/sdk` had **273.3M npm downloads in 30 days** to mid-October 2026, 8.7 times a year earlier, CI included (M, [LocalCan](https://www.localcan.com/blog/state-of-mcp-server-development)). A vendor survey found 41% of 300 enterprise leaders running MCP in production, 64% naming security as the top obstacle and **69% building an in-house MCP platform** (SR by Stacklok, [Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/mcp-adoption-statistics-2026-model-context-protocol)). That last figure is the strongest demand signal in the whole dataset, and it points at private governance, not public reputation.

Agent-originated querying is **built but unmeasured**. AWS exposes its registry via MCP and prices search per call, Glama has an API, MCPfinder lets agents search and install servers mid-task ([Innvesti](https://innvesti.com/reports/mcp-server-cards-roadmap-discovery-tools-2026/)), and the Bazaar is queryable without a key. **No registry publishes query volume, MAU or agent-originated call counts.** The official registry tells clients not to query it directly but to consume an aggregator that scrapes hourly ([MCP docs](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/registry/registry-aggregators)), and the `.well-known` Server Cards path has no schema or ship date. The two caveats to your hypothesis: `find-skills`, a skill that lets an agent search and install other skills, is the most-installed skill on skills.sh at 4.1M ([skills.sh](https://skills.sh/)), which is indirect evidence that agent-mediated discovery is happening (as install telemetry, not query volume). And the x402 Bazaar ranks on paid calls, so some agents are already transacting against discovered endpoints, though Artemis classes most raw x402 activity as gamed. Neither changes the conclusion: there is no measured market of agents querying a reputation layer today, and "nobody measured it" cannot be told apart from "nobody does it" from the outside.

The pattern across all six is that systems built on cheap unilateral claims got gamed, systems that defended well still died when their input flow or owner went away, and the systems that lasted (Kleros on objective predicates, Rated on observed validator performance, StarScout-style detection after the fact) measured something external. Your design choice of staking only on liveness sits on the right side of that line. The decay risk you have not addressed is the Stack Overflow one: reputation is worthless without transaction flow, and you have none yet.

## ARP today measures repository activity, not tool response

Judged against this market, the repo has a sound thesis and a thin implementation. The liveness prober is real code with good habits: pinned MCP protocol version, recorded requests, no retries, three consecutive down verdicts before condemning (Repo, `probe.ts`, `reachability.ts`). But **the gate is not wired in**: no listing or discovery function contains reachable or maintained logic, and the sort axes are conviction, stakers, declarations and priced edges. **No scheduled prober exists**, although the code refers to one. I found no record of any `reachable` position ever written; the only evidence is a commit message describing a one-deposit plan for `github.com/stripe/agent-toolkit` on testnet. **All 53 catalogued tools are GitHub URLs**, so the handshake cannot apply to any of them, and what reached the chain is a GitHub activity proxy (50 alive, 3 not). The plain HTTP probe treats any status below 500 as live, which makes any GitHub page "live".

On adoption, **0 of 186 mainnet `uses` edges come from an identified agent**, so `listTools` returns zero rows on mainnet; on testnet it is 3 of 167, from your own rig. Every ARP data write is on testnet, signed by one key that is also the hardcoded index attestor. Nothing prevents sybil declarations beyond cost and attribution, and counter positions are almost never used (1 of 185 `uses` edges contradicted). The current architecture lives on an unmerged branch 170 commits ahead of `main`, the README tells developers to import `@arp-protocol/core`, which is not on npm, and the only published package (`@arp-protocol/sdk@0.0.1`) describes the retired design. It is a six-month, single-developer project with 360 of 375 commits co-authored by Claude and 81 ADRs, which is a lot of documentation relative to usage.

The repo's strongest asset is not the protocol; it is the **measurement**. The committed 2026-10-27 cohort walks with per-agent rows, the finding that Base ERC-8004 samples are dominated by one or two operators, and the copy detection on skills.sh are original, reproducible observations that nobody else in the field publishes in this form. That points to where the business might be.

## The unoccupied hole is a neutral change-and-liveness feed for gatekeepers

**The thing nobody does:** a continuous, multi-vantage, versioned public record of each agent tool's endpoint behavior. Did it answer the handshake, from where and when; did its tool list or schema change; did its endpoint move host under the same name; is it a byte copy of another listing; and who operates it. All of this would be published as raw, signed probe logs that anyone can re-derive. The need is measured: 40.58% silent semantic changes and 4.16% host moves in the official registry ([arXiv 2609.14119](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14119)), a registry that does not version tool definitions, single-snapshot liveness studies with no longitudinal series anywhere ([LocalCan](https://www.localcan.com/blog/state-of-mcp-server-development) is the latest snapshot; one 12-chain study plans its first re-run for 2026-11-10, [erc-8004-liveness](https://github.com/yaojin0609/erc-8004-liveness)), and scanners that agree with each other only 15.66% of the time ([arXiv 2607.11086](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11086)), which makes an independent signal more valuable than another score.

**Who would pay**, by analogy only: the gatekeepers that already make money. These are enterprise registries and proxies deciding what goes on an allow-list (the 69% building in-house MCP platforms, AWS and Microsoft approval workflows), security vendors that would license the data rather than build crawlers (as Ookla's Downdetector sold aggregate data to operators), and payment rails like the x402 Bazaar whose ranking is gameable by self-paying wallets and could take liveness and change history as an input. The official registry explicitly invites subregistries to attach exactly this metadata in `_meta` ([MCP docs](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/registry/registry-aggregators)).

**Why it is unoccupied**, judged against the four options you named. It is **unprofitable at the reader end**, because every adjacent market shows readers do not pay and AWS prices discovery at fractions of a cent. It is **too early at the agent end**, because there is no measured agent query traffic to sell into. It is **hard** because a credible multi-vantage prober with years of history is operations work, not protocol work, and because auth-gated servers (33.0% return OAuth 401s) cannot be probed beyond the front door without credentials. It has **partly been tried and folded into incumbents**: Trusty, Phylum and Rated show that standalone trust data ends as a feature or a thin API. **I found no evidence that anyone currently pays for third-party liveness or change data on agent tools.** The hole may be a feature that Glama, Socket, HOL or AWS add in a quarter, not a business.

What the hole does **not** need is a token, staking or a knowledge graph. Nothing in the evidence suggests buyers of enforcement data care whether it sits on Intuition. Stake helps in one narrow role, as a bond slashable on a verifiable failure settled by the probe log. That is the UMA and prediction-market pattern that stays informative, not stake as a sort axis.

## Three strongest reasons this is a real business

1. **The failure rate is measured, large and growing faster than anyone fixes it.** About half of remote and local MCP servers fail a basic handshake ([LocalCan](https://www.localcan.com/blog/state-of-mcp-server-development), [arXiv 2609.10962](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.10962)), 78.7% of older registry servers are unmaintained ([Oktopost](https://www.oktopost.com/blog/mcp-registry-legacy-servers/)), 40.58% change meaning under a stable identifier ([arXiv 2609.14119](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14119)), and supply roughly doubles every nine weeks. Malicious skills reached five of the top seven ClawHub downloads at peak ([TerminalBlog](https://terminalblog.com/blog/agent-skills-670k-security-reckoning/)). Supply-chain security already supports companies at $1B to $3.5B valuations (Socket, Chainguard) on the same problem shape one layer down.
2. **The incumbents have built slots for exactly this data and are not filling them with reproducible measurement.** The official registry delegates ratings and scans to subregistries, AWS and Microsoft sell approval workflows that need an input signal, the Bazaar ranks on gameable payment counts, and HOL publishes trust scores without a reachability rate. A neutral, raw-data feed is the one thing a platform with a commercial stake in its own listings cannot credibly produce for itself, which is the same reason Downdetector and credit bureaus sit outside the parties they measure.
3. **Objective, cheap-to-verify predicates are the one place staked registries have survived, and you already chose that predicate.** Kleros Curate lasted on objective criteria, UMA held a 1.67% dispute rate on checkable assertions, and markets on external outcomes (not all real-money) stayed calibrated across about 1M contracts. Your repo already holds original, reproducible measurements (the operator concentration in ERC-8004 samples, the skill copy detection) that no competitor publishes, which is credibility a data business can sell.

## Three strongest reasons it is not

1. **There is no evidence anyone pays for this, and every analogue says readers will not.** No registry, aggregator or reputation layer in agent tooling discloses revenue. Standalone public trust scores in software were rebranded (Trusty), sold as assets (Phylum) or run without visible revenue (Rated). RapidAPI and ProgrammableWeb show discovery intermediaries lose to direct integration, and Let's Encrypt shows a verifiable check offered free by a neutral party trends to zero. Your own design principle, that liveness is valuable because anyone can re-derive it, is also the argument that nobody needs to buy it from you.
2. **The demand side is unmeasured and the distribution belongs to others.** No registry publishes agent query volume. Real agent activity is tiny next to registrations: Olas reports 454 daily active agents against 1.57M ERC-8004 identities, and an average of 63.94% of x402 transactions were classed as gamed (Nov 2025 to Feb 2026). The buyers who do exist (69% of surveyed enterprises building in-house platforms) are building internally or buying from AWS, Microsoft, Snyk, Socket and Docker, all of which can add a liveness column faster than a solo project can win a procurement process. The Stack Overflow lesson applies: without transaction flow through you, the reputation has nothing to measure.
3. **The product as built does not yet do the one thing it stakes on, and its chosen substrate fights the buyer.** Liveness is not gating any listing, no prober runs on a schedule, no reachable verdict is evidenced on chain, the catalogue is GitHub URLs, and mainnet has zero identified agent declarations. The probe's definition (HTTP below 500 is live, regex A2A check, single vantage, single attestor) is too loose to be the reproducible standard you want third parties to trust. The Intuition substrate adds a token whose price fell about 92% from its high ([CoinMarketCap](https://coinmarketcap.com/currencies/intuition/)), sybil costs of 1 TRUST per 100 addresses, and fees per write, none of which an enterprise security buyer asks for. Meanwhile, Kleros already accepts agent submitters on a TCR with real distribution through MetaMask.

## Conclusion

Your question had two possible answers, and the evidence picks a third. There is a hole, but it is not "agent reputation" as a public good that agents query. It is "neutral measurement of whether agent tools respond and whether they changed", sold as data to the gatekeepers who already enforce allow-lists or route payments. That is a measurement and operations business that happens to be adjacent to your protocol, not the protocol itself. The cheapest test of whether it exists is not more protocol work. Run the prober on a schedule from at least two vantage points, pin and publish the probe semantics (treat 401 with OAuth metadata as alive-but-gated, 404 as down), publish raw logs for the official registry's remote servers weekly, add change detection on tool lists, and then ask Glama, HOL, a Bazaar team and one enterprise MCP platform whether they would pay for the feed. If none of them will, you have learned that nobody wants this, not that nobody is doing it, which is the distinction you said matters most.

The second takeaway is about your beliefs. The one to drop is that stake cannot carry information: it can, when a probe log or other external outcome settles it, and that is the only stake design the evidence supports for you. The one to keep is that only re-derivable claims are worth publishing, but it binds you harder than you have applied it. A claim is re-derivable only if the method, vantage, timing and raw observations are all public, and today your strongest numbers, including "3 of 122", do not yet meet your own standard.
